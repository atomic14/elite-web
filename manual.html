ELITE

Space Trader's Flight Training Manual

Issued to Commander Jameson on graduation · Lave Station · Galactic Co-operative of Worlds

Your First Flight

You have a Cobra Mk III, one hundred credits, a pulse laser and no reputation whatsoever. Nobody is going to tell you what to do next — that is rather the point. But here is a first hour that works.

  1. Buy cargo. Press M at the station for the market. Lave is a rich agricultural world, so its food, textiles, liquor and furs are cheap. Buy something and fill the hold.
  2. Pick somewhere to sell it. N opens the short range chart. Anything inside the dashed circle is within your fuel. Industrial worlds pay well for what agricultural worlds grow.
  3. Target it and jump. Move the cursor with the arrow keys, press ENTER to target, then L to launch and H to make the jump.
  4. Fly to the station. You arrive a long way out. Point at the planet and press J for the torus drive; it cuts out near anything solid, including pirates.
  5. Dock. Line up with the rotating port and go in slowly. See Docking — it is the hardest thing you will do today.
  6. Sell, refuel, repeat. And this time buy a little more.

You will also see fat margins on Narcotics and Slaves. Those are illegal. Carrying them makes you a target for the police, and getting caught costs more than the run is worth — until you know what you are doing, and then it is your business.

Your Ship

A Cobra Mk III. Fast, tough enough, and with a hold big enough to make a living. Everything on the console tells you something that will eventually keep you alive.

Fore / Aft shields
Take damage before your hull does, and recharge on their own. They are directional: a ship behind you drains the aft bar. Turning to face an attacker puts a fresh shield between you and it.
Energy
Four banks. What is left after your shields fail — when it reaches zero you are dead. Also fuels the ECM and the energy bomb, so burning it defensively is a real trade.
Fuel
Seven light years, in tenths. Only hyperspace consumes it; flying about a system is free. Refuel at any station, or skim a sun if you have fuel scoops and steady nerves.
Cabin temperature
Rises near a sun. Ignore it and you cook.
Laser temperature
Rises as you fire. Let it max out and the laser cuts out exactly when you need it — fire in bursts.
Scanner
The ellipse at the centre of the console. You are the middle; the stalks show whether a contact is above or below you. Red is hostile, green is not, yellow is the station.

Controls

Two layouts. Classic is the 1984 BBC Micro scheme and the default; Modern is WASD. Toggle with B when docked. Arrow keys fly in both, flight-style — pull back to climb.

These tables are generated from the game's own keymap, so they cannot fall out of step with what your keyboard actually does.

Trading

The whole economy turns on one idea: worlds are short of what they do not make. Every commodity has a price that slides with the local economy, and the two ends of that slide are where your money is.

Buy on agricultural worlds

Food · Textiles · Liquor/Wines · Furs

Grown cheaply where there is land, wanted badly where there is not.

Buy on industrial worlds

Machinery · Computers · Alloys · Luxuries

Made cheaply where there are factories, wanted badly where there are none.

So the classic run is a short hop between neighbours at opposite ends of that scale, carrying one thing out and a different thing back. Poor worlds have the sharpest prices and the worst company.

Contracts (C when docked) pay better than cargo for the same journey — deliveries, courier work, and bounties on pirates. They have deadlines. Missing one costs you the fee and some standing.

Hyperspace & the Charts

N gives the short range chart — your neighbourhood, with a dashed circle showing exactly how far your remaining fuel will take you. G gives the whole galaxy: 256 worlds, all of them real, all of them generated the same way they were in 1984.

Move the cursor with the arrows, ENTER to set your target, D for a full report on the world under the cursor, and F to search by name. Then H in flight to jump.

You arrive at the witch-point, a long way out from the planet, and the journey in is part of the game. That is what the torus drive (J) is for. It disengages near anything with mass — a planet, a station, or somebody who wants your cargo.

Docking

The single most feared manoeuvre in space, and rightly. The station rotates. The docking port rotates with it. You have to arrive lined up with a slot that will not hold still.

  1. Approach the face with the port on it. An amber marker shows where the port is, with an arrow at the screen edge when it is behind you.
  2. Get on the axis — straight out from the port, not off to one side.
  3. Roll to match the port's rotation. This is the part people miss. The opening is a letterbox, and your ship has to be the same way up as it is.
  4. Slow down. Then go in. The marker turns green and reads DOCKING PORT — LINED UP when you have it.

Get it wrong and you bounce off the hull with a bang and a dent in your shields. Get it wrong at speed and you die.

When you have the money, buy a docking computer. It is the least glamorous purchase in the catalogue and the one that will do most for your quality of life.

Trouble

Pirates
They want your cargo, and they size you up before committing — a fat hold and a soft ship draws a crowd. Anarchies are worst.
Jettison (Y)
Throwing cargo out genuinely works. A pirate who gets paid loses interest; this is an escape, not a donation.
ECM (E)
Destroys incoming missiles. Costs energy. A missile you do not answer will take most of your shields with it.
Police
Vipers, and they are good. They care about contraband and about who shot first. Shoot a clean ship near a station and you will find out how good.
The law
Your legal status follows you across the galaxy. Offender is inconvenient; fugitive means every Viper in the system.
Gangs
Organised, well-equipped and coordinated — they fly as a unit rather than a mob. You will not meet one until you are worth the trouble. Consider that a compliment and then consider leaving.

Equipment, roughly in the order worth buying it

  1. Large cargo bay — every run earns more, immediately.
  2. Fuel scoops — free fuel from suns, and lets you collect cargo canisters from things you have shot.
  3. Beam laser — the first upgrade that changes fights.
  4. E.C.M. system — the answer to missiles.
  5. Docking computer — see above. Buy it.
  6. Escape pod — cheap insurance. You lose the cargo and the ship; you keep being alive.
  7. Extra energy unit — faster recharge, which in a long fight is worth more than it sounds.
  8. Military laser, galactic hyperdrive, energy bomb — for when money is no longer the problem.

Stations only sell what their tech level supports. The good things are on the high-tech worlds, which are usually the safe ones.

Ratings

Your combat rating is the only score that matters, and it moves slowly. It starts at Harmless and ends, for almost nobody, at E L I T E.

Harmless · Mostly Harmless · Poor · Average · Above Average · Competent · Dangerous · Deadly · E L I T E

Tougher opponents count for more, so the ladder is climbed by picking harder fights, not more of them. Reaching the top is the whole game. Most commanders die Competent.

The Long Way Out

A story of the eight galaxies

I. Lave

The tree grubs of Lave are the size of a forearm and taste, everyone agrees, of nothing much at all. Kaira Vell had spent four years packing them into crates on the equatorial plantations, and in all that time the only thing she had learned worth knowing was that the crates went up and almost nothing came back down.

She watched them go from the loading yard: a freighter lifting through the rain haze every afternoon at four, dwindling to a bright point, gone. Someone was up there. Someone was always up there, and it was never anybody from the plantations.

The ship cost her everything. It was a Cobra Mk III with a hundred thousand hours on the frame, a pulse laser that had been fitted twice and repaired four times, and a previous owner who had signed the transfer without once looking up. The station clerk gave her a hundred credits of settlement money, a fuel tank filled to seven light years, and a manual she never read.

She had expected to feel something at the transfer. What she felt, mostly, was the specific dread of having no plantation to go back to.

"First ship?" the clerk said.

"Does it show?"

"You're the only one smiling." He stamped the transfer. "Rating, harmless. Don't take that personally, everybody starts there. Some people stay."

II. Diso

She filled the hold with liquor because it was cheap and because the chart said Diso was three and a half light years away and industrial enough to be thirsty. The jump itself was nothing — a lurch, a smear of light, and then a different sun in a different place, hanging there as though it had always been hers to look at.

What nobody had told her was how far out you arrive. The planet sat ahead of her like a coin held at arm's length, and it stayed that size for a very long time. She learned the torus drive from the panel markings, the way you learn anything when there is nobody to ask, and she learned it a second time twenty minutes later when it cut out without warning and dropped her back to a crawl.

The reason it had cut out was drifting a thousand metres off her port side, and it was a Sidewinder, and it was already turning.

Here is what nobody tells you about the first time. It is not frightening in the way you have rehearsed. There is no music. There is a shape on a screen that was not there before, and a small polite chime, and an enormous quantity of time in which to make one decision badly.

She was not brave. She wants that on the record. She hauled the throttle back, opened the cargo hatch, and threw eleven tonnes of decent Lavian brandy into the dark. The Sidewinder broke off to gather it up. It never fired a shot, and neither did she, and she reached Diso Station four hours later with an empty hold, forty credits, and her hands still shaking on the stick.

She sold nothing at Diso, because she had nothing to sell, and spent two days in the station bar working out what she had actually learned. It came to three things. Pirates want the cargo more than they want you. A pulse laser is not an argument. And the reason the old hands all fly the same three routes is not that they lack imagination.

The docking took her six attempts. On the fifth, she understood at last that the station was turning and she was not, and that the letterbox would never come to her — she had to become the same shape as it. On the sixth she went in clean, and the bay lights swallowed her whole, and she sat in the silence afterwards for a full minute before she could unclench her hands.

Afterwards she sat in the bay and laughed until it turned into something else, and then she went and found a terminal and looked up how much a docking computer cost. It was four hundred credits. She had forty. She wrote the number on the back of the transfer papers and carried it around for eight months.

III. The Milk Run

Leesti to Zaonce is three and a half light years and the most boring stretch of space in the eight galaxies, which is exactly why it made her.

Leesti is industrial and poor; Zaonce is industrial and rich and runs the banks that own most of what passes between them. Nobody grows anything on either. So the trade is furs and liquor in one direction and computers in the other, and the margins are thin, and the run is safe, and you can do it four times a week until you have enough money to do something stupid.

Kaira did it for five months. She learned the market the way you learn a language you are not being taught: that prices move day to day, that a world with a poor economy pays more for what it cannot make, that the fat margins on the board at any given moment are usually contraband, and that the two commodities with the best returns in the entire galaxy will get your hold scanned at the first station with police worth the name.

She also learned the thing that is not in any manual, which is that the run is where you practise. Every approach to Zaonce Station was a docking she did not have to survive. By the end of the third month she was going in at speed without thinking about it, matching the roll on the way down the axis, and the six attempts at Diso had become a story she told against herself.

Beam laser. Large bay. Fuel scoops. E.C.M. In that order, because in that order each one paid for the next. She never did buy the docking computer.

IV. Riedquat

Four months later she had a beam laser, a large bay, and a habit of checking the scanner before she checked anything else. She had also acquired the specific arrogance of a pilot who has survived exactly enough to be dangerous to herself.

Riedquat is an anarchy. Everyone knows this. The chart says so, the bulletins say so, and the old hands in the Diso bar said so at some length, which is probably why she went.

They came out of the dark on the way in — three of them, in good ships, spread wide enough that turning to face one meant turning her back on the others. That was the moment she understood the difference between pirates and a gang. Pirates are opportunists who happen to be in the same place. These flew like a single animal with three heads.

She had time to think, with great clarity, that the arithmetic had changed — that everything she knew about pirates had been learned from opportunists, and that these were something else, and that the difference was going to be measured in seconds.

Her aft shield went in the first eight seconds. She rolled, hard, put the fresh face towards them, and ran for the planet with everything the drive had, and the only reason she is alive is that the station's Vipers were already scrambling, and that pirates who have decided the arithmetic has changed are entirely willing to leave.

She docked with her energy in the last bank and a hole where a fuel scoop used to be. The mechanic looked at the damage and then at her.

"Riedquat," he said. Not a question.

"I wanted to see it."

"Everybody wants to see it." He was already writing the bill. "Rating?"

"Competent, as of this morning."

"And you went to Riedquat." He tore off the top copy. "Look. The rating goes up when you take fights you could lose. It doesn't say anything about fights you couldn't win. Those are just dying with extra steps."

He laughed at that — not unkindly, the way you laugh at somebody who has just told you they are halfway up a mountain when you can see the rest of it behind them.

V. Tionisla

There is a graveyard at Tionisla. It is not a metaphor. Two thousand ships in slow orbit, hulls opened to vacuum, name-plates still legible if you fly close enough — and pilots do fly close enough, all the time, because the one thing every commander wants to know is what the end looks like.

Kaira went at the end of her first year, on a whim she could not explain. She took the ship in slow among them, running dark, reading the plates. Traders. Bounty hunters. A Constrictor with its whole spine laid open. A Cobra Mk III much like hers, older, with the name Second Thoughts across the nose, and beside it, welded to the frame by somebody who had known the owner, a plate that just read: SHE WAS COMPETENT, AND THEN SHE WASN'T.

She sat with that for a while.

The graveyard is very quiet. That is the part people leave out of the story afterwards, because it is not dramatic and it is the only thing you actually remember: two thousand ships, absolutely still, and the only sound your own air handling. Every one of them had been somebody who filled a hold at Lave and thought, right, and went.

She thought about the clerk, who had been kind, and had been telling her the truth, and had been wrong about which part mattered. Most commanders make Competent and stop. That is not a warning. It is a description of the exact place where the game either ends or begins, and it is a choice, and almost nobody notices they are making it.

Then she turned the ship around, laid in a course for the far side of the chart, and went looking for the rest of the mountain.

— and that is where you come in.

GalCop Advisory 114-C: Reception Committees

Galactic Co-operative of Worlds · Office of Trade Safety · circulated to all registered hulls

Analysis of eleven thousand incidents finds that piracy is not random. Attackers assess a target before committing, and the assessment is mostly arithmetic: what is aboard, what it would cost to take it, and increasingly, who you are.

Commanders are reminded that a visibly armed ship is attacked less often, and that the deterrent effect of a military laser considerably exceeds its firing value. Commanders of established reputation are advised of the corresponding hazard: a proportion of encounters are no longer attempts on your cargo but attempts on your name. These are better equipped, better coordinated, and will not be bought off with jettisoned goods.

Jettison remains effective against ordinary opportunists. It has no effect on a challenge.

The Tionisla Chronicle — Obituaries

Two hundred and forty-first year of the Co-operative

ORRIN VASSE, of Leesti, at Riedquat, aboard Margin Call. Vasse ran the Leesti–Zaonce circuit for nineteen years without once being fired upon, a record he attributed to leaving before he had to. He is survived by a docking computer he never fitted, on the grounds that he would get round to it. Interment at Tionisla, third ring.

"CAPTAIN" MEHL SORROWAY, of nowhere he would confirm, at Ensoreus. Sorroway claimed on four separate occasions to have flown with the Dark Wheel. The Chronicle notes that on each occasion the claim was made after the third drink and before the bill.

Pilot's log — Second Thoughts, final pages

Recovered at Tionisla. Reproduced by permission of the estate.

Day 402. Zaonce. Fitted the beam laser. Cannot now afford the escape pod. Told myself the laser means I won't need one, which I notice is the sort of thing I would once have found funny.

Day 418. Three of them at Usleti, and they did not come at me one at a time. That is the part nobody writes down. You train against one ship because one ship is what you can imagine. Then there are three and they are all in the wrong place at once.

Day 419. Aft shield still down to a third by morning. Ship's computer keeps telling me the recharge rate is nominal. Nominal is doing a great deal of work in that sentence.

Day 431. Made Deadly today. Nobody to tell.

Notice — Lave Station Trade Board

Posted daily. Do not remove.

Commanders are reminded that narcotics and slaves are prohibited cargo throughout Co-operative space and that ignorance of a hold's contents is not a defence. Scanning is routine within station approach. Penalties escalate with quantity and with prior record.

Commanders are further reminded that the higher margins on prohibited cargo are not a discount offered by the Co-operative but a premium charged by the people who will eventually catch you.

On the Dark Wheel

Collected bar-talk, unverifiable, printed for completeness

Everyone has met somebody who has met somebody. They fly ships that do not appear in any registry. They recruit, supposedly, by watching — never by asking. Every commander who has ever been offered a hyperspace beacon by a stranger in a station bar believes they were being tested, and every one of them tells the story as though they passed.

The Chronicle's position is that the Dark Wheel is what pilots invented so that being very good at this would mean something. The Chronicle's position has never once persuaded anybody.
